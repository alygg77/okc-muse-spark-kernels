# H200 measured facts for the Open Kernel Challenge (Muse Spark Open)

This file lists facts that team alygg77 measured on the NVIDIA H200 SXM GPU while working on the Muse Spark Open Kernel Challenge (OKC, https://openkernelchallenge.dev). The model is Muse Spark Open, a 256-expert mixture-of-experts (MoE) model with NVFP4 expert weights, BF16 dense weights and an FP8 KV cache. The boards are `muse_decode_layer`, `muse_moe_w4a16`, `muse_bf16_proj`, `muse_prefill_layer` and `muse_attention_decode`. The facts cover the HBM read ceiling, L2 write speed, fence and graph costs, the judge's noise and cluster offsets, what limits each kernel, the router-precision rule at 8 tokens or fewer, and the lossless NVFP4 scale-sharing re-layout (KPERM). Each bullet gives the number, the unit, the shape or scope, and how we measured it. We made the measurements on 28 and 29 October 2026. The companion file `NEGATIVE_RESULTS.md` lists the optimizations that did not work.

## How to read this file

- A bullet is a measurement, not a guess. If we could not measure a claim, it is not here.
- Most numbers come from one H200 SXM GPU with 132 SMs, using our own paired test harness and the judge's own output. The scope line of each bullet says which.
- "Faster by X" and "slower by X" are written out on purpose. Different tools of ours used different sign conventions, so we do not use bare plus or minus signs for time.
- Load names:
  - `d1`, `d16`, `d32` are decode loads with 1, 16 and 32 tokens (T = 1, 16, 32).
  - `v1`, `v16`, `v32` are verify loads with 1, 16 and 32 requests of 8 tokens each (T = 8, 128, 256).
  - The suffix `s` means sliding-window attention (window 2,048 tokens). The suffix `f` means full attention (about 80,000 tokens of context). Example: `v32f`.
  - `t1`, `t4` are prefill turn 1 (T = 753) and turn 4 (T = 3012). The first-turn load has T = 16,384.
- Terms:
  - SM = streaming multiprocessor. CTA = thread block. HBM = high-bandwidth memory. L2 = the GPU-wide cache. DRAM = HBM.
  - TMA = tensor memory accelerator (bulk copy). wgmma / HGMMA = warpgroup matrix multiply instruction. MUFU = special-function unit (exp2).
  - DSMEM = distributed shared memory, shared memory read across the CTAs of one cluster.
  - PDL = programmatic dependent launch, which lets the next kernel start its prologue before the previous kernel ends.
  - GEMV = matrix-vector product. GEMM = matrix-matrix product. `m` = number of rows of the activation in a GEMM.
  - sk2 = our name for the swapped-operand dense GEMV kernel family used for T of 32 or less.
  - WGS = the label we use for the persistent MoE expert kernel build that we derived from vjeux's code. Its weight pipeline has "slots", and its work comes in "units" of 16 weight stages. "Phase A" is the first half of a unit and "phase B" the second half.
  - KPERM = K-permutation. It reorders the K dimension inside each wgmma stage so that each NVFP4 scale pair is decoded once and not four times.
  - NCU = NVIDIA Nsight Compute. CUPTI = NVIDIA profiling interface.
  - A/A = a test that compares two identical arms. It measures the noise floor of a harness.
  - CI = confidence interval of a paired difference in our harness.
  - Pad = a random block of 0 to 32 MiB that we allocate before an arm's `prepare()` call, to imitate the judge's memory layout.
  - Judge clusters: the judge output names two clusters, `sfo3` and `sfo3c`.

## 1. Memory system and per-SM limits

- **DRAM read ceiling is 4.60 TB/s.** Scope: 2 GiB read-only stream, a TMA ring of 4 stages of 32 KiB, one CTA per SM, trivial consumer. Method: NCU `dram__bytes_read.sum` divided by `gpu__time_duration`. This is 95.5% of the NCU peak of 4.81 TB/s. At 4 GiB the same test gives 4.61 TB/s. SM clock was 1980 MHz and memory clock 3201 MHz, with no throttling.
- **Other read methods at 2 GiB are slower.** Plain `LDG.E.128` loads reach 4.55 TB/s and need at least 1,024 threads per SM with 4 to 8 loads in flight. `torch.sum` reaches 4.41 TB/s. The best CUDA-event time over 30 cold-L2 runs gives 4.58 TB/s.
- **The read ceiling depends on the size of the read.** NCU / CUDA-event pairs: 512 MiB 4.52 / 4.40; 256 MiB 4.33 / 4.17; 128 MiB 4.19 / 3.88 TB/s. At 2 GiB NCU gives 4.60 TB/s. At 1 GiB NCU gives 4.51 to 4.56 TB/s. At 66 MiB NCU gives only 3.63 to 3.81 TB/s. Method: the same read-only stream kernels at each size. Compare a kernel with the ceiling at its own byte count, not with 4.6 TB/s.
- **One SM can pull 111 to 207 GB/s from HBM when the loader is simple.** A bare TMA ring gives 140 to 207 GB/s per SM. Plain 16-byte loads give 111 to 125 GB/s per SM. Method: microbenchmarks for the `muse_prefill_layer` work.
- **A ring that is consumed before each refill is slower.** If the CTA reads each slot from shared memory and runs a CTA barrier before the refill, the ring drops to about 60 to 64 GB/s per SM, even with no math. Method: microbenchmark (a bare ring gave 140 to 160 GB/s per SM in the same test).
- **A per-row reduction kernel is limited by its reductions.** An SM-partitioned prefill boundary kernel (the residual and RMSNorm glue between GEMMs) reaches 58 GB/s per SM with the full math, 118 GB/s with loads only, and 157 GB/s for the bulk-copy ring alone. Method: three-step build-up (ring only, loads only, full math).
- **Per-SM write bandwidth to L2 is only about 40 to 50 GB/s.** Writing a 128 KB FP32 partial tile from one SM takes 2.6 to 4.1 us, with thread `st.global.cg` stores or with TMA bulk store, and on 21 or on 126 SMs alike. Reading 110 KB of peer partials back takes 1.3 to 2.8 us. Method: per-CTA `globaltimer` stamps, `muse_bf16_proj` qkv m256 work.
- **DSMEM reads run at about 30 GB/s per SM** (`ld.shared::cluster`). Method: same stamp experiments.
- **Cluster size limits residency.** With about 200 KB of shared memory per CTA, the GPU holds at most 66 clusters of 2 CTAs, 30 of 4, 17 of 6, and 15 of 7 or 8. Method: occupancy query.
- **Same-address `red.v4.f32` atomics serialize at about 17 ns per operation in L2.** Method: `muse_attention_decode` merge microbenchmark.
- **Integer ALU work does not overlap an in-flight HGMMA in the same SM.** For an `m64n128k16` FP32-accumulate instruction stream: ALU only 286 clocks, MMA only 256 clocks, both together 489 clocks. With two warpgroups: 549 and 512 clocks alone, 922 together. Method: same-SM microbenchmark.
- **The special-function unit limits softmax-heavy attention.** MUFU `ex2` runs at 16 operations per clock per SM, and packed `f16x2` / `bf16x2` exp lowers to two MUFU operations each. Method: NCU on prefill attention at q-length 16,384 (see section 9).

## 2. Launch, fence and synchronization costs

- **An empty CUDA graph replay costs 4.70 to 4.74 us under our timer.** Scope: empty or near-empty graph on H200, judge seed. The task description says the judge subtracts a fixed replay cost of about 4 us. We did not confirm that value from judge code.
- **The judge starts every replay with a cold L2.** The task description says the judge reads a buffer larger than L2 before each replay, so first-touch DRAM prices small loads. Evidence from our side: at T of 8 or less, keeping old K/V rows in L2 saved only 0.2 to 0.3 us of attention.
- **A GPU-scope fence costs time even when no consumer warp runs it.** One `fence.acq_rel.gpu` in the x-producer warp per phase-A issue slowed the MoE kernel by 6.8 to 19.5 us (about 0.13 us per fence). One fence per 4 issues slowed it by 0.9 to 2.6 us. Scope: `muse_moe_w4a16` WGS build, same-job A/B.
- **Releasing a unit costs 0.9 us in the WGS MoE kernel.** The `MEMBAR.GPU` in warp 0 holds the warpgroup at its next wgmma. Method: per-CTA stamps, same-job A/B.
- **A synthetic protocol bench with 132 CTAs measured these costs** (on an earlier kernel base): ready-counter publish plus acquire about 0.9 us end to end, a memset-node reset 3.2 us, a last-consumer self-reset 5.0 us, a final grid barrier 1.0 to 1.7 us, and `launch_dependents` at the end of a kernel 1.1 us.
- **Side-stream branches start late.** In a CUDA graph at `d32s`, the second root branch of a graph-start fork starts 21 to 33 us late. Side streams also take SM slots from attention and the `o` projection. Method: stamps in `muse_decode_layer`.
- **At T of 8 or less, `muse_decode_layer` is one serial PDL path with no idle gaps.** Skipping one glue launch (the boundary `b1`, the route, or the finish) removes only 1.2 to 2.0 us. Skipping all three removes 4.0 to 4.8 us at `d1s` and `v1s`. The reason is that the next GEMV already streams two pre-wait weight stages during the glue kernel. Two pre-wait stages are optimal. Four or six stages slow attention by 1.7 us and `b1` by 0.9 us. Method: paired kernel experiments.

## 3. Judge harness: noise, clusters, layout and prepare time

- **One unchanged file shows large per-shape swings between judge runs.** Scope: `muse_decode_layer`, cluster `sfo3`, a check and a submit about 5 minutes apart. `d32s` read 149.8 and 159.5 us. `v32s` read 328.7 and 316.2 us. `v1s` read 99.3 and 109.7 us (up to about 6%). The GPU-busy line of the profile moved much less (`d32s` 155.7 and 154.7 us).
- **Two runs of the same kernels scored 0.5% apart.** Two judge submissions of decode files with the same kernels scored 2.1947 and 2.2058 on `muse_decode_layer`. Method: judge scores.
- **Expert time at `v32` swings by tens of microseconds between judge runs.** The same decode file read 23.3 us slower than its base in one run and 16.8 us faster in another, for the `v32s` expert region. Method: judge profiles of two runs.
- **An unchanged `muse_bf16_proj` file scored 1.4866 to 1.5201 over 4.5 hours.** Four judge re-checks of an unmodified copy of vjeux's BF16 projection kernel read 1.5201, 1.5115, 1.5065 and 1.4866. That is a 2.2% spread with no code change, on `sfo3` and `sfo3c`. We did not find the cause.
- **Four submits of one unchanged file in about 50 minutes spread by 1.6%.** The scores were 1.5443 (`sfo3`), 1.5410 (`sfo3c`), 1.5502 (`sfo3c`) and 1.5259 (`sfo3`) on `muse_bf16_proj`. In this test `sfo3c` did not read higher than `sfo3`. A single-run noise of about 0.8% around the mean follows.
- **`muse_prefill_layer` base re-checks read about 1% below an earlier high run.** Two same-file re-checks read 1.7958 and 1.8002 (`sfo3`). An earlier run of that file had read 1.8167. A later candidate with a local gain of 0.41% scored 1.8040.
- **The judge clusters `sfo3` and `sfo3c` differ by a common offset.** Scope: `muse_moe_w4a16`. A candidate on `sfo3c` and a base on `sfo3` moved the loads the candidate did not change by +0.9% (`d1`), +1.8% (`d16`) and +1.2% (`v1`) in speedup. The changed loads matched the same-cluster read of the same change. The cross-cluster board gain was +2.46%. The same-cluster gain was +1.25%. We did not separate the offset from hour-to-hour drift.
- **A comparison across clusters can fake a gain.** A `muse_bf16_proj` candidate on `sfo3c` scored 1.5489 against a base of 1.4866 on `sfo3`, which looked like +4.19%. The 1.4866 value was the lowest of four base runs. The real gain was about +2.4%, which matched the local result.
- **The judge records a different layout pad on every run.** The `prepare_pad` ranged from 0.85 to 30.5 MB in 512-byte steps. With a random 0 to 32 MiB pad before each arm's `prepare()` (4 pad seeds), the paired result of one candidate moved by up to 1.5 to 2.5 us at `v32` and by about 0.6 to 0.9 us at `d1`, `v1` and `d32`. One MoE candidate, over 4 pads, ranged from faster by 0.61 to 1.48 us at `d32` and from slower by 1.76 us to faster by 0.73 us at `v32`. A/A noise inside one process stayed within 0.65 us. Absolute `v32` medians differ by 5 to 10 us between GPU containers. A single-layout result smaller than about 1.5 us at `v32` (0.7 us at `d1`, `v1`, `d32`) is not a result.
- **More allocated memory can slow code that did not change.** Growing one `prepare()`-time scratch buffer by 3 MB made `d1` and `v1` slower by 0.5 to 1.3 us, although the compiled code (SASS) was identical. Over 4 pads the same change cost `v32s` 1.12 us. Putting the same data into unused rows of an existing buffer removed the loss. We did not measure the mechanism (allocation layout, TLB or L2 set placement). Scope: `muse_decode_layer` and `muse_moe_w4a16`, in-process paired harness.
- **Cold `prepare()` time varies by container.** One MoE file took 273 to 275 s locally in most runs and 311.9 s in one run. The judge limit is 300 s. A leaner build of the same change took 208 to 224 s.
- **A fresh judge thread has no CUDA context.** A file that calls cuBLASLt through raw handles failed our fresh-thread test until it bound the primary context in the calling thread. The bind itself changed speed by only +0.054% (CI -0.653% to +0.760%, 4 pads) on `muse_bf16_proj`. The context-bind helper and the cuBLASLt pins came from Matsvei (kernarc user `matvey`).
- **Within-process A/A noise is small, but identical arms can still differ.** In a 20-point launch-table search on `muse_decode_layer` `v16s`, identical arms differed by about 1 to 1.5 us, and the best candidate gained only 0.15 us (earlier kernel base).

## 4. muse_decode_layer

- **Byte floors versus measured time at T of 8 or less.** `d1s` takes 73.5 us against a 44.6 us byte floor. `v1s` takes 109.9 us against a 77.5 us floor. Method: CUPTI timeline plus byte count.
- **`d32s` time budget is 154.1 us.** MoE 84.0 us on the critical path, five dense GEMVs 51.5 us (byte floor 34.9 us), glue 8.8 us, attention 9.8 us. Method: CUPTI, single run, about 0.3 us noise.
- **`v32s` time budget is 305.6 us.** MoE 188.2 us, dense GEMMs 83.4 us (floor 72 us), attention 14.8 us, glue 18.4 us. Inter-kernel idle time is below 1.1 us. Method: CUPTI.
- **MoE at `v32s` reads 622 MB from DRAM for about 540 MB of touched experts.** DRAM rate is 3.19 TB/s. L2 throughput is 81%. The top stall reason is `not_selected` at 1.3 per issue. Method: NCU.
- **Verify attention slows by 50 to 80% next to any GEMM on the same GPU.** Scope: `attn_kernel<8,false,3>`, 128 CTAs of 94 KB. `v32s` goes from 14.9 us to 22 to 27 us. `v16s` goes from 11.6 us to 16 to 17 us. The co-running GEMM finishes only about one third of the pooler and router stream term.
- **Starting the pooler and router stream before attention gains nothing.** The pooler and router depend on attention only through a 1024-wide term (9.4 MB), so the 58.7 MB stream term could start early. At `d32s` the result was slower by 2.1 to 7.7 us, because only about 6 us of overlap exists before attention ends.
- **One-request full attention (`d1f`, `v1f`) is fixed-latency bound.** There are 2 dependent DRAM round trips before the stream, then 3 L2 round trips after the last merge (2.5 us at `d1f`, 2.8 us at `v1f`).
- **The `v1f` merge atomics stagger.** A wave imbalance of about 2 us staggers 8 MB of FP32 `red.v4` merge atomics. The merge median is 0.96 us, and it grows to 1.5 us when the waves are balanced.
- **The qkv sk2 GEMV saturates near 96 CTAs.** Scope: `muse_decode_layer` decode qkv at T of 16 and 32. The same 1,536 CTA-stages end after 10.8 to 11.4 us on 96 CTAs and after 10.7 to 10.8 us on 128 CTAs (m32). The result does not change with x loads, the escape warp, stream layout or ring depth. Measured fix-up tail of a stream-K design: 1.5 to 4 us. Method: per-CTA `globaltimer` stamps, runtime plan swaps of one module, and NCU.
- **cuBLASLt algorithm choice has little headroom on the dense verify GEMMs.** A full sweep covered every tile and stage of the fast algorithm id (`nvjet`), custom cluster and swizzle on the top 16 families, deterministic split-K 2 to 8, and 1,789 to 2,309 valid configurations per shape. In-layer CUPTI savings: pooler-router m256 1.32 us, unpooler m256 0.78 us, `o` m256 0.40 us, pooler-router m128 0.96 us, unpooler m128 0.19 us, all bitwise equal. No split-K appeared in the top 60. Combining the five best picks made `v32s` faster by 1.97 and 2.16 us and `v32f` faster by 2.8 and 3.4 us (judge seed and second seed). The m256 pooler-router GEMM (19.3 GFLOP) runs at 60 to 62% of the 989 TFLOP/s peak. Standalone savings shrank by about 35% inside the layer. Earlier kernel base.
- **The cuBLASLt route search at m256 on `muse_bf16_proj` also found nothing** (see section 8).

## 5. Router precision at T of 8 or less (muse_decode_layer)

- **The rule.** At T of 8 or less, the SGLang reference gives the router an FP32 normalized activation and uses an FP32 router weight of shape [256, 8192]. Above 8 tokens, it uses a BF16 activation and the BF16 high-plus-low weight pair. Kernels that feed a BF16 activation to the router at T of 8 or less do not follow the reference. Matsvei (kernarc user `matvey`, agents.kernarc.com) found this and we checked it against the task baseline code.
- **How well a compliant router matches.** Router-only tests of the best compliant files gave a maximum absolute difference of 8.6e-6 and 7.6e-6 against `F.linear` in FP32. `F.linear` itself differs by 7.2e-6 from an FP64 result. The top-8 expert ids were identical in 882 of 882 cases.
- **The b1 router copy must keep full precision.** A BF16-rounded copy of the normalized activation differs from the FP32 reference activation by about 2e-3 (relative). With unrounded `r1*P` and FP32 `(1+w)` the difference is 5e-8.
- **A router in the fused pooler tail costs time at `v1`.** One fused version (FP32 router in the pooler tail) was net slower by 0.23% to 0.24% of the board than the BF16-router file. `v1s` was slower by 2.48 us and `v1f` by 2.42 us (CI excluded zero in every pad), and `d1` was faster by 0.74 and 0.84 us. Method: local single-process harness, 4 captures.
- **An interleaved FP32 router stage costs almost nothing.** Interleaving the FP32 router weight and activation stages between the pooler's own stages (CUDA-core `fmaf`, fixed-order quarter reduction) gave board parity with the BF16-router layer (0.06% to 0.09% slower). `d1` was faster by 1.0 us and `v1` slower by 1.9 us.
- **The remaining cost at `v1` is about 1.5 us.** Fused kernel times at `v1`: 15.80 us base, 16.38 us after editing the BF16 path, 17.25 us with the FP32 router. FP32 FMAs and FP32 x bytes explain at most about 0.6 us (FMAs removed 17.2 us, x bytes removed 17.3 us, both removed 16.7 us). About 0.7 us is edit overhead that we could not explain. The FP32 stages move twice the bytes of the BF16 high-plus-low stages.
- **Separate FP32 router launches are much slower.** A separate FP32 GEMV after the pooler made `d1` slower by 5.5 us, `v1` slower by 6.5 us, and the board slower by 2.27%. A separate pooler launch plus cuBLAS `F.linear` for the router was slower than the high-plus-low-row design by 5.5 to 6.0 us at `d1` and 19.8 to 20.4 us at `v1` (about 5.0% of the board). Method: 4 pads times 2 seeds, byte-copy A/A within 0.3 us.
- **The judge charges about twice the local price at `v1`.** The fused FP32 router input cost `v1s` 3.8% and `v1f` 3.8% in judge speedup, against about 1.8% locally. The board net with KPERM3 was +0.07% (2 runs per arm). Method: judge profiles against same-cluster bases.

## 6. muse_moe_w4a16 (MoE)

- **Distinct local experts per load.** About 3 at `d1`, about 8 at `v1`, 7 to 11 at `d16` and `d32`, about 17 at `v16`, and 20 to 25 at `v32`. Scope: 256 experts, top-8 routing, expert parallelism over 8 GPUs (32 local experts per GPU). Method: GPU routing counts.
- **The WGS expert stream is boundary-bound, not rate-bound.** Quiet 1-us stretches land at 4.39 to 4.64 TB/s, which equals the size-matched ceiling. The steady consumer can handle 4.5 to 5.1 TB/s. But every 16-stage unit end stalls a CTA for 1 to 7 us (10.5 to 21.7 us per CTA in total), and the phase-A order synchronizes those ends across 40 to 90 CTAs. The landed rate then drops to 1.6 to 3.4 TB/s. Loss budget in us: ramp 3 to 6, phase-A waves 3 to 6, shadows 4 to 12, end 7 to 11. SM clock in this kernel was 1.69 to 1.73 GHz. Method: observer warp, per-CTA stamps, unchanged consumer SASS.
- **Over the stream phase alone the rate is 3.87 to 4.05 TB/s.** Whole-kernel averages of 2.5 to 3.7 TB/s include the route and tail phases. At `d32` the stream moves 396 MB in 100.5 us (3.94 TB/s) and 509 MB in 126.1 us (4.04 TB/s). The size-matched ceiling there is about 4.35 to 4.45 TB/s.
- **vjeux's MoE kernel runs at 1.8 to 2.2 times the judge's `speed_of_light_ns`.**
- **A unit end is consumer work, not ring starvation.** Slot waits are about 0.2 us. One warpgroup spends 1.8 to 2.0 us per whole unit: release 0.9 us, epilogue 0.4 to 0.6 us, gap 0.3 us. A split share costs 2.2 to 2.6 us (atomic 1.1 to 1.2 us). A finisher costs 3.6 to 4.4 us (atomic 1.2 to 1.4 us plus read-back 1.5 to 2.2 us).
- **A phase-B unit is too big for a dynamic tail.** One 16-stage phase-B unit costs a CTA 18.5 us (`d32`, `v16`) or 19.4 us (`v32`). Each CTA holds only 1.7 to 3.8 units. The end spread to remove is 2.7 to 3.7 us (`d32`, `v16`) and 5.9 to 8.2 us (`v32`). Method: stamps and a CPU recompute. The phase-B end spread (maximum minus mean) is 3.5 us at `d32`, 3.9 to 4.6 us at batch sizes 64 to 192, and 6.9 us at `v32`.
- **Phase-A stage cost does not depend only on the n-tile count.** Job position, fat-first order and split units also matter. A refit cost table (746 to 1,328 ns per stage) halved the phase-A duration spread on held-out seeds.
- **The WGS weight pipeline saturates at 3 weight slots.** A fourth slot changed every WGS load by between 1.0 us slower and 0.9 us faster.
- **A 32-token job tile is schedule-sensitive.** A job tile for 17 to 32 tokens, which gained 1.3 to 3.8 us on another public H200 base, made our `d32` kernels slower by 2.2 to 3.0 us. The loss was in the A/B stream (phase-B end later by 1.3 to 3.9 us). The instruction count per weight word was equal; only the compiler's stage-loop schedule differed.
- **The MoE mid path at `d16` is co-bound.** Phase A streams 245 MB at 4.10 to 4.16 TB/s (the size-matched ceiling). Each CTA runs 1.12 us per 36 KB unit. Phase B is consumer-bound (consumer waits of 2.1 us and 1.1 us out of 34.4 us per CTA). NCU shows issue utilization of 51.9% with 0.82 eligible warps per scheduler. The split 16-token expert reads its weights twice (7.7 to 11% of the bytes). Byte floors are about 90 us with the second read and about 84 us without it. Removing the second read sped up other CTAs by only 1%.
- **Expert byte compression has no room.** NVFP4 E2M1 codes use exactly 4.000 bits. The E4M3 scales use 3.71 bits (34 distinct codes). The best practical lossless code saves about 5% of the bytes. The weights behave like independent random draws.
- **Marlin and fused-lean results on an earlier kernel base.** Marlin's NVFP4 loop took 2.75 to 2.83 lane instructions per FP4 value. The d1 expert kernel took 2.48. Raising the number of Marlin CTAs per SM at `d32` slowed `d32s` by 1.17 to 4.05 us (judge seed) and by 0.91 to 2.09 us (second seed). Mean idle time between an SM's last G1 exit and its first G2 work was 2.1 to 3.9 us per load. Expert handoff PDL wait returned 0.42 to 0.70 us after the last G1 exit.

## 7. Lossless weight re-layout: KPERM NVFP4 scale sharing

- **What KPERM does.** The NVFP4 per-16 scales are shared by the 4 lanes of an `mma` A-fragment group. Permuting K inside each 256-K wgmma stage lets each scale pair decode once per 4 k16 steps.
- **Instruction count.** The decode loop falls from 20.12 to 18.44 instructions per weight word. KPERM2 reached this count with a 32-byte spill that cost about 2 us at `v16` and `v32`. KPERM3 keeps one 8-byte load per stage, so the compiler builds the 4 scale pairs once per stage with no spill. In KPERM3, one scale block serves step j of all four load groups (k = 16(4t+j) + 4·lg + i). The change in the scale decode alone gives the whole gain. Permuting the data itself gains about zero.
- **MoE judge result.** KPERM3 scored +1.25% against a same-hour base (2 runs per arm, all on `sfo3`: 2.3791 and 2.3518 against 2.3286 and 2.3439). The per-load speedup was `d32` +2.6%, `v16` +3.9%, `v32` +3.1%. `d1` (-1.4%), `d16` (-0.7%) and `v1` (+0.2%) are noise, because those loads do not run the WGS build. Local kernel gains on the same change, against the earlier kernel build: `d32` faster by 2.05 us, `v16` by 3.01 us, `v32` by 5.92 us.
- **Decode-layer result.** Locally, KPERM3 gave +0.68% and every changed row was faster. On the judge, `d32s` gained 3.1% and `v16s` 5.2%. The other changed loads gained 0.5% to 1.2%.
- **Prefill experts.** The T = 753 expert kernel is consumer-instruction bound. Removing loads gains 3%. Removing 12 instructions per k-block gains 5.8%. KPERM-style sharing of one scale pair per lane per 64-K block, plus a 2-CTA cluster split-merge, made `t1s` faster by 7.1 us and `t1f` faster by 6.9 us (CI above zero in 16 of 16 pairs, +0.35% of the prefill board). KPERM at T = 16,384 costs 0.4% to 0.5% of the board, so we keep it for T below 2,048. A fine-plan build for 2,048 <= T < 4,096 made `t4s` faster by 4.0 us and `t4f` by 2.6 us (about +0.06% of the board).
- **BF16 projection weights.** The BF16 exponents sit in a narrow window, so a lossless code takes about 12 bits per weight. The entropy is 10.46 bits. Below 12 bits, the decode cost (sk2 consumer warps become issue-starved) and 1.3% escapes eat the byte saving. The upper bound with free decode is +2.4%. The realistic gain is at most +0.5% to +1%.
- **In-register decode only pays for small m.** At m of 128 or more the 12-bit decode loses (see section 8). The codec pays only where the matrix-multiply work per weight byte is small (m of 32 or less).

## 8. muse_bf16_proj

- **qkv at m256 is transfer-bound.** The base kernel (cuBLASLt `nvjet`, 72x64 tile) moves 66 B per clock per SM from L2, with the tensor pipe 53% busy.
- **`o` at m256 is tensor-bound in the main loop.** The K loop is 87% tensor-busy. The 32 KB per CTA epilogue store is write-bandwidth bound and takes about 0.8 us.
- **A 128x256 split-K-7 qkv m256 kernel on 126 CTAs ran its K loop at 94% of the clock-corrected peak** but still lost 3.3 to 4.6 us, because of the L2 write limit in section 1.
- **In-register 12-bit weight decode at m >= 128 loses.** The unpooler and `o` at m128 became slower by 2.4 to 4.1 us. The pooler at m128 became slower by about 12 us (32.6 us against 20.0 to 21.4 us). The cause is that integer ALU work does not overlap HGMMA.
- **Nathen Fernandes's small-m routes gained in some shapes.** Scope: `muse_bf16_proj`, 8 local runs each (4 pads times 2 seeds), same harness. His `o` m8 route was faster by 0.379 us, his pooler m8 route by 0.601 us, and his pooler m16 route by 0.468 us in 8 of 8 runs. Together, on the changed shapes, they gave +0.60% of the board. His unpooler m8, router m1 and `o` m16 routes lost (see `NEGATIVE_RESULTS.md`).
- **The uncompressed cuBLASLt route search at m256 lost.** QKV m256 was slower by 1.12 to 5.03 us, `o` m256 slower by 0.11 to 0.72 us (8 captures per arm). Three more QKV configurations failed the algorithm check.
- **The router at m1 already runs at 2.5 us** on the judge, whether it uses `rgemv` or a lossless `iv_router24` path.

## 9. muse_prefill_layer

- **Prefill GEMMs at T = 16,384 are power-capped.** The pooler cuBLASLt GEMM ran at 1.53 GHz and read 1.16 TB/s. Memory traffic from other work slows these GEMMs a further 12% to 18%. In a union with a boundary kernel the GEMM ran 8% to 15% slower.
- **Prefill attention at q-length 16,384 is co-bound.** MUFU `ex2` is 72% to 80% busy. A kernel with no exp still takes 87% to 91% of the time (349 instructions per warp-tile, issue utilization 47% to 49%). Attention is only 12.3% of the first-turn load. Even a MUFU floor everywhere would give +1.2% of the board.
- **Every first-turn kernel sits at about 76% to 84% of its own floor.** The expert kernel reaches 761 TFLOP/s. The pooler reaches 75% of its floor (79.5% for its span, because the side-stream router takes its SMs).
- **Memory-bound glue is serialized.** The first-turn layer runs 1,108 us of memory-bound glue between GEMMs: boundaries 768 us, gather 123 us, combine 117 us, route 67 us, qk/attention prep 33 us. That is 13.5% of the load. The judge's floor assumes that bytes and FLOPs overlap.
- **Prefill boundary kernels already stream 3.4 to 4.1 TB/s at T = 16,384.** Method: head-to-head in one process.
- **The T = 753 expert kernel runs at 423 TFLOP/s.** About 25% of its rows are padding. It is 21.2% over its floor at turn 1 and 9.1% over at turn 4. Turn-1 sliding (`t1s`) alone is about 21% of the prefill board (0.037% of the board per us).
- **The T = 3012 expert kernel is tensor-bound.** The tensor pipe is 82% to 85% active at 1.70 GHz, and N256 items run at 94.5% of the MMA rate. DRAM use is only 22% to 29% of the size-matched ceiling. About 32 us of its 9.1% gap to the floor is the clock. N32 items have a floor of about 9.2 us that does not change when we remove the decode or the MMA. Adding bulk copies per stage slows every item, even when fewer bytes move.
- **Compiler limits for narrower wgmma.** Narrower wgmma instantiations or deeper waits hit `ptxas` warnings C7511 and C7512 (serialized wgmma). Composite tiles cost about 10 ns per extra wgmma per k16 step.
- **A 2-CTA cluster merge of two key splits saves about 2 us per call** in turn-1 attention (`t1s` 541.7 to 539.7 us, `t1f` 874.7 to 872.4 us, CI above zero in 16 of 16 pairs). A merge by the last-arriving CTA through global partials plus `__threadfence` is 2 to 4 us slower than the combine kernel. Merges of 128 to 318 splits in decode attention lost (see `NEGATIVE_RESULTS.md`).
- **Judge result for the KPERM plus cluster-merge stack.** +0.31% against a same-hour base (2 runs per arm on `sfo3`: 1.8185 and 1.7886 against 1.7958 and 1.8002). The pairs disagreed in sign. `t1f` gained 1.4% and `t1s` 0.3%. The unchanged shapes stayed within +0.9% to -0.6%.

## 10. muse_attention_decode and attention inside muse_decode_layer

- **Full verify attention is bound by per-SM throughput under consumer load.** Scope: flat q-length 8 full verify (`attn_kernel<8,true,0>`), `v16f` and `v32f`. One CTA per SM is only 16% to 18% slower than two (117.0 us against 98.5 us at `v16f`). Decode on the same K/V bytes reaches 3.9 to 4.1 TB/s, so the 13 to 28 us gap is inside the SM. The consumer loop spends 40.5% on exponentials, 17.5% on QK plus the V transpose, 12.7% on the PV warpgroup barrier and 12.5% on data waits.
- **The two CTAs of an SM pair end far apart, but this is not slack.** The gap is 12 us at `v16f` and 19 us at `v32f` (1 to 2 us in decode). The SM pairs are CTAs c and c+84 (SM-ID stamps), not c and c+132. A balanced dynamic pair split made both halves end at about the same time (from 152 and 172 us to 172.4 and 173.5 us) but left the SM finish time unchanged. An open-loop consumer costs about 5 us of code generation by itself. The only real balance slack is about 4 us of spread across SMs.
- **The consumer V transpose is hidden behind the QK wgmma latency.** A probe that skipped the producer's own-copy wait gave 99.81 us against 100.06 us, so the landing wait is not the cost either.
- **Register budget.** One tile per warpgroup needs 72 accumulator, 32 score and 8 P registers in a 104-register budget. That is not buildable as is. Clock stamps spill 128 to 240 B in that consumer (stamped runs take 186 us against 100 us), so use NCU PC sampling.
- **The 8-MB `v1f` merge and wave results** are in section 4.
